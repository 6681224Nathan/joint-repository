1. Thévenin and Norton

From two terminals A and B, any network of sources and resistors acts like one battery in series with one resistor (Thévenin), or one current source in parallel with the same resistor (Norton). They are the same circuit in two outfits.

QuantityHow to get it
VthRemove the load. Measure the open-circuit voltage between A and B.
InPut a wire across A–B. Measure the short-circuit current.
RthSwitch all sources off, then look into A–B. (Or Rth = Vth / In.)
Switching a source off: a voltage source becomes a wire. A current source becomes a gap.

Example from last time (12 V, 33 kΩ, 10 kΩ divider): Vth = 2.79 V, Rth = 33k ∥ 10k = 7.67 kΩ, In = 2.79 V / 7.67 kΩ = 0.364 mA.

2. Mesh vs nodal (same circuit, both ways)

You know both methods. This shows how to choose between them.

Mesh (2 unknowns, I1 and I2 clockwise)Nodal (1 unknown, VA)
Left: 10 = 1·I1 + 2(I1 − I2) → 3I1 − 2I2 = 10
Right: 3·I2 + 4 + 2(I2 − I1) = 0 → −2I1 + 5I2 = −4
Solve: I2 = 0.727 mA, I1 = 3.82 mA		 KCL at A (currents leaving):
(VA − 10)/1k + VA/2k + (VA − 4)/3k = 0
VA = 6.18 V

Check: current in R2 = 6.18 V / 2k = 3.09 mA, and I1 − I2 = 3.09 mA ✓ (units: V, kΩ, mA).

Choosing: count the unknowns. Here nodal needs 1 and mesh needs 2, so nodal is quicker. Mesh is better when there are many series loops. The professor solves the diode examples with mesh (you'll see it in S1).

3. Capacitors

SignalThe capacitor acts like
DC (steady)an open circuit (gap)
Fast AC, big Ca wire (short circuit)

How big its "resistance" is for AC: Xc = 1 / (2π f C). Example: 100 µF at 1 kHz gives Xc = 1.6 Ω, which is a wire next to resistors of several kΩ.

Why this matters for amplifiers: a coupling capacitor lets the AC signal in and blocks the DC bias. A bypass capacitor across RE removes RE for AC only. DC analysis: delete the capacitors. AC analysis: replace them with wires.

4. Sine-wave numbers

Why this part exists: rectifier questions start from the wall socket or a transformer, which give a sine wave. The question usually tells you one number about it (like "120 V RMS"), and you need to turn it into the number the circuit actually uses (the peak). That's all this part is.

A sine voltage goes up and down smoothly, forever. It's written as v(t) = Vp·sin(ωt). Only two things describe it: how high it goes and how fast it wiggles. Every other number is one of these two in a different form.

NumberPlain meaningExample: 17·sin(314t)
How high
Vp (peak)The top of the wave. The number in front of "sin".17 V
VrmsThe "equivalent DC" value: the battery voltage that would heat a resistor equally. Vrms = 0.707 × Vp. Meters and wall sockets show RMS.0.707 × 17 = 12 V
How fast
f (frequency)Full up-and-down cycles per second (Hz). Thai mains is 50 Hz.314 / 6.28 = 50 Hz
T (period)Time for one cycle. T = 1/f.1/50 = 20 ms
ω (omega)The same speed in a different unit (rad/s). ω = 2πf. It's the number next to t.314 rad/s
The two conversions you'll actually use:
1) RMS → peak: Vp = 1.414 × Vrms. "220 V mains" → 311 V peak; "120 V RMS" → 170 V peak. Diodes and capacitors charge to the peak, so always convert first.
2) ω → f: f = ω / 2π. You need f for the ripple formula in S2.

Practice (try first, then open the solution)

P1 · Thévenin and Norton (about 8 min)

Look into terminals A–B. RL (dashed) is not connected for a–c.

  1. Find Vth.
  2. Find Rth (switch the 12 V source off).
  3. Find the Norton current In, and check In = Vth/Rth.
  4. Connect RL = 4 kΩ across A–B. Find its current and voltage.
Solution P1

a) With A–B open, no current flows in R3, so X is a plain divider: Vth = 12 × 6/(3+6) = 8 V.

b) The 12 V source becomes a wire: R1 ∥ R2 = 3·6/9 = 2 kΩ, in series with R3: Rth = 2 + 2 = 4 kΩ.

c) In = 8 V / 4 kΩ = 2 mA. Check with A–B shorted: R2 ∥ R3 = 1.5 kΩ, VX = 12 × 1.5/(3+1.5) = 4 V, I = 4 V / 2 kΩ = 2 mA ✓.

d) IL = 8 / (4k + 4k) = 1 mA, VL = 1 mA × 4k = 4 V.

P2 · Nodal and mesh (about 8 min)

  1. Use nodal analysis to find VA.
  2. Find I1 (through R1) and I2 (through R3).
  3. Confirm with the mesh equations.
  4. Does the 3 V source deliver or absorb power? How much?
Solution P2

a) KCL at A: (VA − 12)/2 + VA/3 + (VA − 3)/6 = 0. Multiply by 6: 3VA − 36 + 2VA + VA − 3 = 0, so 6VA = 39, VA = 6.5 V.

b) I1 = (12 − 6.5)/2k = 2.75 mA, I2 = (6.5 − 3)/6k = 0.583 mA. Current in R2 = 6.5/3k = 2.17 mA, and 2.75 = 2.17 + 0.583 ✓.

c) Mesh: 5·I1 − 3·I2 = 12 and −3·I1 + 9·I2 = −3. Check: 5(2.75) − 3(0.583) = 12 ✓; −3(2.75) + 9(0.583) = −3 ✓.

d) I2 enters the + terminal of the 3 V source, so it absorbs 3 V × 0.583 mA = 1.75 mW. (The 12 V source delivers 33 mW, resistors use 31.25 mW, and 33 − 1.75 = 31.25 ✓.)

P3 · Sine wave and capacitor (about 5 min, no figure)

A transformer secondary gives v(t) = 17·sin(314t) V.

  1. Find f and T.
  2. Find Vrms.
  3. What is the peak voltage of a 220 V RMS wall socket?
  4. Find Xc of a 100 µF capacitor at this frequency and at 1 kHz. Which acts more like a wire?
Solution P3

a) ω = 314 rad/s, so f = 314/2π ≈ 50 Hz, T = 1/f = 20 ms.

b) Vrms = 17/√2 = 12.0 V.

c) Vp = 220 × √2 ≈ 311 V.

d) 50 Hz: Xc = 1/(2π·50·100×10⁻⁶) = 31.8 Ω. 1 kHz: Xc = 1.59 Ω. The 1 kHz case acts more like a wire. Both are small next to kΩ resistors.

Common mistakes: forgetting to switch the source off for Rth · using Vp where a question wants Vrms · wrong source sign in mesh equations.
