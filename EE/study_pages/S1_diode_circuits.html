1. What a diode does

A diode is a one-way valve for current. Current can flow from the anode (A) to the cathode (K), in the direction the triangle points. It blocks current going the other way.

A real silicon diode needs about 0.7 V across it before it really conducts (germanium: about 0.3 V). That 0.7 V is the barrier potential of the PN junction (theory in T2).

2. The three diode models

A diode isn't a resistor, so you can't put it straight into KVL or mesh equations. The trick is to swap the diode for something simple that KVL understands (a wire, a small battery or a gap), then solve as usual. Which swap you use is called the model. The question tells you which one ("ideal diode" or "VD = 0.7 V" / "non-ideal").

What this table is for: it's the toolkit for the method in section 4. When you guess a diode is ON or OFF, the middle columns tell you what to draw in its place (step 2). After solving, the last two columns tell you how to test whether your guess was right (step 4). Find your model's row and use only that row.

3. Load line and Q-point (slide 29)

Why this exists

The ideal and 0.7 V models are shortcuts. A real diode doesn't jump on at one voltage; it follows a curve. So "what are VD and ID?" has two unknowns, and two unknowns need two equations: one from the circuit and one from the diode. The diode's equation has an exponential in it, so you can't solve it by hand easily. The load line solves the pair with a graph instead of algebra.

The circuit (slide 29)

EquationOn the graph it is…
1. From the circuit (KVL around the loop)VPS = ID·R + VD
5 = ID·2k + VD		a straight line: the load line
2. From the diodeID = IS(eVD/nVT − 1)the diode's curve (given to you)

Every point on the line obeys the circuit. Every point on the curve obeys the diode. Only the crossing obeys both, so that crossing is the real answer. It's called the Q-point (quiescent or operating point).

Step by step

Shortcut for any load line, in two points:
Point 1: pretend the diode is a wire (VD = 0). The current is the biggest it can be: I = VPS/R. Mark it on the current axis.
Point 2: pretend the diode is a gap (ID = 0). No current, so no voltage on R; all of VPS sits on the diode. Mark VPS on the voltage axis.
Join them with a ruler, and read where the line crosses the curve.

Now play with it

Move the sliders and watch three things:

  • Bigger VPS: the line slides outward (same slope) and the Q-point climbs the curve. More current.
  • Bigger R: the line gets flatter (it pivots around VPS) and the current drops.
  • Whatever you do, VD stays around 0.55–0.67 V. That's why the 0.7 V model works so well.

4. Method: assume, then check

You can't know in advance whether a diode is ON or OFF, so you guess and then check. This is exactly what the professor does in the notes.

  1. Guess each diode: ON or OFF. (A good guess: anode connected toward the + of a source → probably ON.)
  2. Replace each diode using the table in section 2 (middle columns): ON → wire (ideal) or 0.7 V battery; OFF → gap.
  3. Solve the normal circuit with KVL, mesh or nodal (S0).
  4. Check every diode using the table's last two columns. ON diodes: is ID > 0 (flowing anode → cathode)? OFF diodes: is VA − VK below 0 (ideal) or below 0.7 V?
  5. If a check fails, flip only that diode and solve again. Repeat until every check passes. Only then are the numbers the answer.
Trap: a negative ID doesn't mean "current flows backwards". A diode can't do that. It means your guess was wrong and that diode is OFF.

Practice (try first, then open the solution)

P1 · Ideal diode (lecture 3, slide 2) · about 6 min

  1. Is the diode ON or OFF? Prove it with assume-and-check.
  2. Find the voltage at node N.
  3. Find the current through the 10 Ω resistor.
Solution P1

Try 1: assume D ON (wire). KCL at N: (10 − V)/4 = V/10 + (V − 12)/15. Multiply by 60: 150 − 15V = 6V + 4V − 48, so V = 198/25 = 7.92 V. Diode current = (7.92 − 12)/15 = −0.272 A < 0 ✗. So D is OFF.

Try 2: D OFF (gap). Simple divider: VN = 10 × 10/(4 + 10) = 7.14 V. Check: anode 7.14 V, cathode side sits at 12 V (no current in 15 Ω), so VA − VK = −4.86 V < 0 ✓ OFF.

Current in 10 Ω = 10/14 = 0.714 A.

Lesson: the 12 V on the cathode side is higher than anything the 10 V side can push, so the valve stays shut.

P2 · Two diodes (lecture 2 assignment, slide 34) · about 10 min

  1. Ideal diodes: find the state of D1 and D2, ID1, ID2 and VN.
  2. Repeat with the 0.7 V model.
Solution P2

(a) Try 1: both ON. D2 is a wire, so VN = 3 V. ID1 = (10 − 3)/4k = 1.75 mA ✓. Current down 6k = 3/6k = 0.5 mA. KCL at N: 1.75 + ID2 = 0.5, so ID2 = −1.25 mA ✗ → D2 OFF.

Try 2: D1 ON, D2 OFF. Divider: VN = 10 × 6/(4 + 6) = 6 V, ID1 = 10/10k = 1 mA ✓, ID2 = 0. Check D2: anode 3 V, cathode 6 V → 3 − 6 = −3 V < 0 ✓.

(b) 0.7 V model, D2 OFF: 10 = 4k·I + 0.7 + 6k·I → ID1 = 0.93 mA, VN = 0.93 × 6 = 5.58 V. D2: 3 − 5.58 < 0.7 ✓ still OFF.
