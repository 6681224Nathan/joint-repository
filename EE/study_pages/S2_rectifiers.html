1. The big picture: wall AC → steady DC

Phones, laptops and LEDs need steady DC, but the wall gives 220 V AC (a sine wave). A DC power supply does the conversion in four stages (slides 4, 29). The quiz focuses on stages 1 to 3.

StageJob
1. TransformerSteps 220 V down to a smaller AC voltage (e.g. 12 V).
2. Rectifier (diodes)Flips or blocks the negative half, so the output is always positive. Still very bumpy.
3. Filter (capacitor)Fills the gaps between bumps, leaving a small wobble called ripple.
4. Regulator (e.g. 7805)Removes the last ripple and holds an exact voltage (7805 → 5 V).

2. Transformer (1 minute)

A transformer has two coils. The voltage ratio equals the turns ratio: Vprimary / Vsecondary = N1 / N2. Example: 220 V with a 10:1 transformer gives 22 V RMS on the secondary.

Ideal diodes on this page. Your professor's notes treat diodes as ideal (ON = plain wire, no 0.7 V drop), so every formula and problem here does too. Only subtract 0.7 V per ON diode if a question says "silicon diode" or "0.7 V".
Always do this first: transformer voltages are RMS. Diodes and capacitors care about the peak. So: Vm = 1.414 × Vsecondary, RMS. In this page, Vm means the peak of the secondary voltage.

3. Half-wave rectifier (slides 5–10)

One diode in series with the load. It lets the positive half through and blocks the negative half. Press the button to switch half-cycles. The green band is where current flows.

Why π: the average of one positive sine bump spread over a whole cycle is Vp/π. You don't need to derive it, just use 0.318.

PIV (peak inverse voltage) is the largest reverse voltage the diode must survive while it's OFF. You pick a diode whose rating is above it.

4. Full-wave: center-tapped (slides 11–16)

The transformer has a wire from the middle of the secondary (the center tap). Each end of the coil goes to its own diode, the two cathodes join at node J, and the load sits between J and the center tap. D1 works on the positive half, D2 on the negative half, and both push current through RL in the same direction. So you get a bump on every half-cycle.

7. Capacitor filter and ripple (slides 9–10, 16, 21–22)

A capacitor across RL works like a water tank. Near each peak, the diode fills it up to Vp (the charge path). Between peaks the diode is OFF, and the tank slowly drains through RL (the discharge path). The output drops a little, then gets refilled at the next peak. That small saw-tooth wobble is the ripple Vr.

What the circuits look like with a capacitor

The capacitor C always goes in parallel with the load RL (across the output). Nothing else changes. Press the button to switch between the two phases: charging (near each peak, the diode is ON and fills C) and discharging (between peaks, every diode is OFF, so C alone feeds RL).

Remember fr: half-wave fr = f (50 Hz). Full-wave fr = 2f (100 Hz). Using the wrong one is the most common ripple mistake. Vr = Vmax − Vmin (peak-to-peak), as in your class notes.

8. Design example (30 V, 300 mA, your professor's working)

Slide (30 V supply): Design a rectifier/filter giving about 30 V at up to 300 mA, fed from 120 V AC RMS, 60 Hz. Ripple less than 10% of the output at full load. Bridge rectifier, ideal diodes.
StepWorking
1. The loadRL = V / I = 30 / 0.3 = 100 Ω. Bridge = full-wave, so fr = 2 × 60 = 120 Hz.
2. RippleVr ≤ 10% of 30 V = 3 V.
3. Peak VmVdc = Vm − Vr/2 → 30 = Vm − 1.5 → Vm = 31.5 V.
4. CapacitorVr/Vm = 1/(frRLC) → 3/31.5 = 1/(120 × 100 × C) → C = 875 µF (a real part: 1000 µF, rounding up).
5. TransformerPrimary peak = 120 × 1.414 ≈ 170 V. Turns ratio = 170 : 31.5 (≈ 5.4 : 1). Secondary RMS = 31.5/1.414 = 22.3 V.
6. DiodesBridge PIV = Vm = 31.5 V, current 0.3 A → e.g. 1N4001 (1 A, 50 V).

Some versions use "10% of Vm" instead of 10% of the output: then Vr = 0.1Vm and Vdc = Vm − 0.05Vm = 0.95Vm. Read which one the question means.

9. Theory you may be asked (slides 23–25, 29–31)

Half-waveFull-wave
AdvantagesSimple, cheap, easy to useHigher efficiency; no half of the signal is wasted; less ripple
DisadvantagesLow output power and voltage; higher ripple; poor transformer utilizationMore parts; bigger; more expensive
ApplicationsLow power: signal demodulation, peak detectionPower supplies, chargers for phones and laptops, UPS, LEDs and motors, welding, radio signal amplitude detection
  • Center-tapped vs bridge: center-tapped needs a special transformer and its diodes face 2Vm. The bridge needs 4 diodes (and with real diodes loses 1.4 V instead of 0.7 V), but works with any transformer and its diodes only face Vm. extra
  • 7805 regulator (slide 29): transformer → bridge of 4 × 1N4001 → 470 µF capacitor → 7805 → steady +5 V. The 0.1 µF capacitors remove high-frequency noise.
  • Dual supply (slide 31): one bridge plus two regulators (LM340 for +, LM320 for −) gives both + and − voltages.

P2 · Bridge with filter (quiz style) · about 12 min

Mains 220 V RMS, 50 Hz. Transformer 15:1. Bridge of ideal diodes. C = 1000 µF, RL = 500 Ω. Draw the circuit, then find: (a) Vm, (b) Vp(out), (c) ripple frequency, (d) Vr, (e) Vdc and the load current, (f) ripple factor, (g) PIV, (h) Vdc if C is removed.

Solution P2

Circuit (a bridge rectifier with filter: transformer, 4 diodes, C in parallel with RL):

a) Secondary RMS = 220/15 = 14.67 V → Vm = 20.74 V.

b) Ideal diodes: Vp(out) = Vm = 20.74 V.

c) Full-wave: fr = 2 × 50 = 100 Hz.

f) r = 0.415/20.53 = 0.020 (2%).

g) Bridge: PIV = Vm = 20.74 V.

h) No C: Vdc = 0.636 × 20.74 = 13.19 V. The capacitor raised the DC output from 13.2 V to 20.5 V.

P4 · Design (slide 27, your professor's working) · about 10 min

Slide 27: a load rated 0.2 W at 5 V must be powered by a half-wave rectifier from 220 V mains (50 Hz). Take the ripple as 10% of Vm (Vr = 0.1Vm) and 5 V as the DC output. Draw your design (with values on it), then find: (a) RL, (b) Vm, (c) C, (d) the turns ratio, (e) PIV.
Solution P4

Circuit (half-wave + filter, with the values found below):

a) P = V²/R → RL = 5²/0.2 = 125 Ω.

b) Vdc = Vm − Vr/2 = Vm − 0.05Vm → 5 = 0.95Vm → Vm = 5.263 V.

c) Half-wave: fr = 50 Hz. Vr/Vm = 1/(frRLC) → 0.1 = 1/(50 × 125 × C) → C = 1.6 mF (1600 µF).

d) Primary peak = 220 × 1.414 ≈ 311 V. Turns ratio = 311 : 5.263 (≈ 59 : 1).

e) Half-wave with C: PIV = 2Vm = 10.5 V.

Bridge version (the professor also showed this): fr = 100 Hz, so 0.1 = 1/(100 × 125 × C) → C = 800 µF. Vm is still 5.263 V, and PIV = Vm = 5.26 V.

P5 · "What is the output waveform?" (slide 33) · about 8 min

vi is a sine with 10 V peak. Ideal diodes, all resistors 2 kΩ. Sketch vo and give its peak. (Hint: do the positive half and negative half separately, using S1's assume-and-check.)

Solution P5

Name the corners T (top, input +), B (bottom, input −), L (left) and R (right). D1 goes L→T and D2 goes T→R.

Positive half (T higher): D2 ON (anode T is high). D1 OFF (its cathode T is high). Current: T → D2 → R, then splits to B: directly through R–B (2k), or R → middle 2k → L → L–B 2k (4k). VR = vi, and L sits halfway down the 4k path: VL = vi/2. So vo = VR − VL = +vi/2.

Negative half (B higher): D2 OFF, D1 ON (L→T conducts). Take T = 0, so L = 0 (D1 is a wire) and B = |vi|. From B to L: direct 2k, or B → R → L (2k + 2k), so VR = |vi|/2. vo = VR − VL = +|vi|/2.

Result: vo is positive on both halves, a full-wave rectified wave with a 5 V peak (half of 10 V because of the resistor divider).

Common mistakes: forgetting RMS → peak · using f instead of 2f for full-wave ripple · subtracting 0.7 V when the question uses ideal diodes · using the whole secondary as Vm in a center-tapped rectifier · mixing up "10% of Vm" and "10% of the output" for the ripple.
