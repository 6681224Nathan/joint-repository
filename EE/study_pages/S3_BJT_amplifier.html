1. What a transistor is

You know a diode: a one-way valve for current. A transistor is a valve you can control. Think of a water tap: turning the small handle a little controls a big flow of water. In a BJT (Bipolar Junction Transistor):

  • a small current into the base (the handle) controls
  • a big current from collector to emitter (the water flow).

That gives two big uses: a switch (handle fully off or fully on, e.g. a tiny microcontroller current turning a motor on) and an amplifier (a small wiggle on the handle makes a big copy of the wiggle in the flow). Your quiz is about the amplifier.

A BJT is three layers of semiconductor. In an NPN (the one in your course) it's N, then a thin P, then N. The arrow in the symbol sits on the emitter and points the way current flows: out of the emitter for NPN. (A PNP is the mirror image: P-N-P, arrow pointing in, all currents and voltages reversed.)

2. How it works inside (slides L4 5–9)

Look at the layers as two diodes back to back: one between base and emitter (the BE junction) and one between base and collector (the BC junction).

  1. BE junction forward biased (base about 0.7 V above emitter, exactly like a diode that is ON). The emitter is heavily doped, so it pours electrons into the base.
  2. The base is very thin and lightly doped. Only a few electrons find a hole to recombine with there. Those few make the small base current IB.
  3. BC junction reverse biased (collector higher than base). Its electric field grabs almost all the electrons arriving in the base and sweeps them into the collector. That is the big current IC.

So a small change in VBE (or in IB) causes a big change in IC. The mode where BE is forward and BC is reverse is called the active region, and it's the one used for amplifying (slide L4 9).

Why 0.7 V again? The BE junction is just a silicon diode. When the transistor is working, VBE ≈ 0.7 V, the same as a diode that is ON. That's why every BJT calculation starts with "VBE = 0.7 V".

3. The three currents and β (slide L4 16)

Current goes in at the base and the collector, and everything comes out of the emitter (KCL on the transistor as one node):

RuleMeaning

Example: β = 100 and IB = 20 µA. Then IC = 100 × 20 µA = 2 mA, IE = 2.02 mA, α = 100/101 = 0.990.

4. The three modes (slides L4 10–12, L5 2–7)

A transistor is always in one of three states. Think of the water tap again: closed, partly open, or fully open. Which state it's in depends on its two inside "diodes" from section 2: the BE junction and the BC junction. Each one is either ON (forward biased) or OFF (reverse biased).

ModeLike a tapBE diodeBC diodeHow to spot it (NPN)
Cutoffclosed: nothing flows (open switch)OFFOFFVBE < 0.7 V, so IB = 0 and IC = 0
Activepartly open: the flow follows the handleONOFFVC > VB > VE, and IC = βIB. Amplifiers work here.
Saturationfully open: can't flow any more (closed switch)ONONVC < VB, VCE ≈ 0.2 V, and IC is less than βIB

How to find the mode in a problem. Same trick as diodes: guess, solve, check.

  1. Guess "active". Calculate IB, then IC = βIB, then VCE.
  2. Check: is VCE bigger than 0.2 V? Yes → the guess was right, it's active.
  3. No (VCE came out below 0.2 V, or negative) → the guess was wrong, it's saturated. Set VCE = 0.2 V and find IC from the resistors instead of from βIB.
  4. If VBE can't even reach 0.7 V (the base supply is too small), it's cutoff: all currents are 0.

5. DC biasing: setting the Q-point (slides L4 21–30)

Biasing = choosing resistors so that, before any signal arrives, the transistor rests in the active mode with a sensible IC and VCE. That resting point is the Q-point from section 4. There are two common bias circuits:

Fixed bias (slide L4 24)

One resistor RB goes from VCC to the base. Walk from VCC, through RB, into the base and down to the emitter (KVL): VCC = IBRB + 0.7, so IB = (VCC − 0.7)/RB. Then IC = βIB and VCE = VCC − ICRC.

Problem: IC depends directly on β, and β changes a lot from one transistor to the next (and with temperature). Swap the transistor and the Q-point moves.

Voltage-divider bias (slide L4 28, your amplifier circuit)

Two resistors, R1 and R2, split VCC to set the base voltage, and RE sits under the emitter. KVL is awkward here because current splits at the base (some goes into the base, the rest down R2). So first replace R1 and R2 with their Thévenin equivalent: one battery VTH plus one resistor RTH (see S0). Then a single KVL loop gives IB:

StepFormulaWhy

Worked example: Exercise 3 (slide L4 28)

VCC = 12 V, R1 = 33 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ, RC = 2.2 kΩ, RE = 1 kΩ, β = 100.

  1. VTH = 12 × 10/43 = 2.79 V; RTH = 33 × 10/43 = 7.67 kΩ.
  2. IB = (2.79 − 0.7)/(7.67 k + 101 × 1 k) = 2.09/108.7 k = 19.2 µA.
  3. IC = 100 × 19.2 µA = 1.92 mA; IE = 101 × 19.2 µA = 1.94 mA.
  4. VE = 1.94 mA × 1 k = 1.94 V; VB = 1.94 + 0.7 = 2.64 V; VC = 12 − 1.92 mA × 2.2 k = 7.77 V.
  5. VCE = 7.77 − 1.94 = 5.82 V > 0.2 V → active ✓ (and VC > VB > VE).
  6. Load line: IC(sat) = 12/3.2 k = 3.75 mA, VCE(cutoff) = 12 V. Q-point (5.82 V, 1.92 mA).
  7. With β = 200: IB = 2.09/(7.67 k + 201 k) = 10.0 µA → IC = 2.00 mA. β doubled, but IC changed only 4%. That's why voltage-divider bias is preferred: RE makes it stable.

6. The amplifier idea: the common-emitter amplifier (slides L5 8–14)

Now we use the biased transistor to make a small signal bigger. Here's the idea in four steps:

  1. The DC bias holds the transistor at its resting point, in the middle of the active mode.
  2. A small AC signal is added at the base, so the base voltage wiggles a tiny bit.
  3. The collector current follows that wiggle, but β times bigger.
  4. That bigger current flows through RC, so the voltage at the collector wiggles a lot. That's the amplified output.

The name common emitter (CE) means the emitter is shared by the input side and the output side (for AC it's connected to ground). The input goes into the base; the output comes out of the collector.

PartJob
R1, R2voltage divider that sets the DC base voltage (bias)
RCturns the collector current wiggle into a voltage wiggle (this is where the gain comes from)
REstabilises the DC bias against changes in β and temperature
C1 (input coupling)lets the AC signal in but blocks DC, so the source doesn't upset the bias
C2 (output coupling)passes the AC output to the load but blocks the DC collector voltage
CE (bypass)a short circuit for AC across RE: RE works for DC but disappears for the signal, so the gain stays high
Rs, RLthe signal source's own resistance and the load being driven (not part of the amplifier)
The big trick: solve the circuit twice, once for DC and once for AC.
The circuit has two kinds of voltage in it at the same time: the steady DC from VCC, and the small AC signal. Handling both at once is messy, so we split them and handle each on its own (this is superposition, from S0):
1. DC analysis (section 6b): only VCC is on. Capacitors are open (gaps). Find IC and check the transistor is active.
2. AC analysis (sections 7–9): only the signal is on. Capacitors are wires, VCC becomes ground, and the transistor is swapped for the hybrid-pi model. Find the gain, Zi, Zo.
Always do DC first: the AC model needs the IC that DC gives you. This matches your class notes.

What the signal looks like

The input is a small sine (±10 mV). The output is the same shape, about 124 times bigger (practice P4's numbers), and upside down.

Why upside down? Base goes up → IC goes up → more voltage drops across RC → less voltage is left at the collector, so the collector goes down. That flip is the minus sign in every CE gain formula (also called a 180° phase shift).

Both graphs show only the wiggle. The real voltages are the DC value plus the wiggle: for example, the collector really swings up and down around its DC voltage VC. If the wiggle is too big, or the Q-point is too close to an end of the load line, the tops or bottoms get flattened ("clipped", slide L5 9).

6b. DC analysis of the CE amplifier, step by step

Before the transistor can amplify anything, you need its resting current IC (the Q-point). Finding it is the DC analysis, and it's always part (a) of an amplifier question. Here is the whole thing on one amplifier, slowly, with every step explained.

Example circuit (same layout as the figure in section 6): VCC = 15 V, R1 = 47 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ, RC = 4.7 kΩ, RE = 1.2 kΩ, RL = 10 kΩ, Rs = 600 Ω, β = 150, all capacitors large.

Part 1: get the DC circuit (click through)

Step 1: the full amplifier. It has many parts, but for DC most of them don't matter. The next step shows which ones drop out.

Step 2: the DC rule: every capacitor is an open gap (blue). Why: a capacitor is two metal plates with an insulator between them. Once it has charged up, steady DC current can't cross the insulator, so for DC it's just a break in the wire. What that does here (faded = cut off for DC):

  • C1 open cuts off the signal source and Rs. (The signal is AC anyway, so for DC it's off.)
  • C2 open cuts off the load RL. No DC flows into the load, so RL is not in the DC analysis.
  • CE open does nothing to RE: DC still has to go through RE. So RE stays in the DC analysis.

Step 3: what's left is exactly the voltage-divider bias circuit from section 5: VCC, R1, R2, RC, RE and the transistor. From here on it's the section 5 method.

Step 4: replace R1 and R2 by their Thévenin equivalent: one battery VTH and one resistor RTH feeding the base. Now there are two simple loops: the base loop (left: VTH → RTH → base → emitter → RE → ground) and the collector loop (right: VCC → RC → collector → emitter → RE → ground).

Common mistakes in the DC part
• Putting RL or Rs into the DC calculation. They're cut off by C2 and C1.
• Leaving RE out because "CE shorts it". That's only true for AC. For DC, CE is open and RE counts.
• Forgetting the (β + 1) in front of RE. RE carries IE, which is (β + 1) times IB.
• Using VTH as VB. They differ by IBRTH (here 2.632 vs 2.548 V), because the base current drops a little voltage across RTH.
• Skipping the active-mode check. It's usually worth marks ("verify the transistor is in the active mode").

Shortcut (only when the question allows an approximation): if βRE ≥ 10R2, the base current is tiny compared with the divider current, so VB ≈ VTH and IE ≈ (VTH − 0.7)/RE. Here 150 × 1.2 k = 180 k ≥ 100 k, so it's allowed, and it gives IE ≈ 1.61 mA (5% high). Your quiz 1 solution used the exact base loop, so use the exact method unless told otherwise. For a ± supply (R2 and RE going to −VEE), see Midterm prep, section 3.

7. The hybrid-pi model (slide L5 13)

For the AC part you want to use KVL and Ohm's law, but you can't do that with a transistor symbol in the way. So you swap the transistor for a few simple parts that act the same way for small wiggles. It's the same idea as swapping a diode for a wire or a 0.7 V battery. For the transistor, the swap is called the hybrid-pi model. The exam gives you the drawing; you need to know what each part means and the two formulas (gm and rπ).

PartWhat it meansFormula
rπ (B to E)The base side. For small wiggles, the BE junction behaves like a plain resistor, and rπ is that resistor.rπ = β / gm
vπThe AC voltage across rπ, i.e. between base and emitter. It's the "handle" that controls the output.emitter grounded: vπ = vin
gmvπ (C to E, diamond)The collector side: a current source whose current is set by vπ (the diamond shape means "controlled source", from lecture 1). It's the tap's flow, controlled by the handle.gm = IC / VT = IC / 26 mV
gmHow much collector current you get for each volt of vπ. It uses the IC from the DC analysis, which is why DC comes first. VT ≈ 26 mV at room temperature (some books use 25 mV).unit: siemens (A/V), e.g. 1 mA/26 mV = 38.5 mS
ro (C to E)A very large resistor (it comes from the Early effect, slide L4 20). Usually ignored (treat it as a gap) unless the question gives a value.ro = VA/IC (VA = Early voltage)

Only if the h-parameters on slides L5 11–13 come up: they're the same model with different names. h11 ≈ rπ (input resistance), h21 ≈ β (current gain), h22 ≈ 1/ro, h12 ≈ 0.

Quick check that it works: IC = 1 mA, so gm = 38.5 mS. A 10 mV wiggle on the base gives gmvπ = 38.5 mS × 10 mV = 0.385 mA of collector wiggle. Through RC = 5 kΩ that's 1.9 V. 10 mV in, 1.9 V out: a gain of about 190.

8. Building the AC equivalent circuit, step by step

This is where most students get lost, so click through it one step at a time. It's the AC version of what section 6b did for DC.

Step 1: the full amplifier. The DC analysis is already done (section 6b), so you know IC and can work out gm and rπ.

Step 2: apply the AC rules (blue = what changed).

  • Every capacitor becomes a wire (C1, C2, CE). Big capacitors let AC straight through. (This is the opposite of DC, where they were gaps.)
  • VCC becomes ground. It's a steady battery, so it has no wiggle, and for AC "no wiggle" means 0 V.
  • CE is now a wire across RE, so the emitter connects straight to ground and RE no longer matters (faded).

Step 3: tidy up. R1's top end used to go to VCC, which is now ground. So R1 and R2 both go from the base to ground: they're in parallel (R1∥R2). The same thing happens at the collector: RC (to "VCC" = ground) and RL (to ground through the C2 wire) are in parallel: RC∥RL.

Step 4: swap the transistor for the hybrid-pi model. rπ goes from B to E (which is ground), and the current source gmvπ goes from C to E. Now it's a normal circuit, and the answers come out in a couple of lines (section 9).

Recipe for any CE amplifier question

  1. DC (section 6b): capacitors open. VTH, RTH → IB → IC, IE → VE, VB, VC → VCE. Check it's active.
  2. AC model values: gm = IC/26 mV, rπ = β/gm.
  3. Draw the AC circuit (section 8): capacitors → wires, VCC → ground, transistor → hybrid-pi. Drawing it usually earns marks.
  4. Read off the answers: Zi, Zo, Av, then Avs and Ai if asked.

11. Theory you may be asked (midterm)

ConfigurationInputOutputCommon (grounded for AC)Known for
Common emitter (CE)basecollectoremitterhigh voltage gain, inverted output
Common collector (CC, emitter follower)baseemittercollectorgain ≈ 1, high input / low output impedance (a buffer)
Common base (CB)emittercollectorbaselow input impedance, current gain α ≈ 1
  • BJT vs FET (slide L4 2): transistors split into BJT (NPN, PNP) and FET (JFET, MOSFET). A BJT is current-controlled (base current); a FET is voltage-controlled.
  • Pros (slide L4 13): large amplification factor, good voltage gain, works in four regions (active, saturation, cutoff, reverse), good high-frequency response, can act as a switch.
  • Cons: sensitive to heat, can produce noise, lower switching power than FETs.
  • Input characteristic (IB vs VBE, slide L4 19): looks exactly like a diode curve, turning on near 0.7 V.
  • Output characteristic (IC vs VCE): one nearly flat line per IB (the active region), squeezed together near VCE ≈ 0 (saturation), and IB = 0 along the bottom (cutoff). The slight upward slope of the lines is the Early effect, modelled by ro.
  • Switch use (slide L4 4, 26): Vin = 0 → cutoff → lamp OFF. Vin high → saturation → lamp ON. For a guaranteed ON, βIB must exceed IC(sat).

Use VBE = 0.7 V, VCE(sat) = 0.2 V, VT = 26 mV, ro ignored, unless told otherwise.

P1 · Which mode? · 3 min

Each line gives node voltages of an NPN. Name the mode. (a) VB = 0.7, VE = 0, VC = 5 V. (b) VB = 0.3, VE = 0, VC = 12 V. (c) VB = 2.7, VE = 2.0, VC = 2.2 V. (d) VB = 3.0, VE = 2.3, VC = 8 V.

Solution P1

a) VBE = 0.7 (ON) and VC > VB > VE → active.

b) VBE = 0.3 V < 0.7 V → BE junction OFF → cutoff.

P2 · Fixed bias (Exercise 1, slide L4 24) · 6 min

β = 100. Find (a) IB, (b) IC and VCE, (c) the load line endpoints and the mode, (d) the mode if RC is changed to 6 kΩ.

Solution P2

a) KVL around the base loop: IB = (12 − 0.7)/565 k = 20.0 µA.

b) IC = 100 × 20 µA = 2.0 mA; VCE = 12 − 2 mA × 3 k = 6.0 V.

c) IC(sat) = 12/3 k = 4 mA, VCE(cutoff) = 12 V. Q-point (6 V, 2 mA) is right in the middle → active.

d) Assume active: VCE = 12 − 2 mA × 6 k = 0 V < 0.2 V. Impossible, so it's saturated: VCE = 0.2 V and IC = (12 − 0.2)/6 k = 1.97 mA (less than βIB = 2 mA).

P3 · Voltage-divider bias (class assignment, slide L4 30) · 10 min

β = 100. Find (a) VTH and RTH, (b) IB, IC, VCE and the mode, (c) the load line endpoints, (d) the power dissipated in the transistor at the Q-point, (e) the % change in IC if β = 200, (f) a standard RC that gives VCE ≈ 5 V.

Solution P3

a) VTH = 10 × 4.7/19.7 = 2.39 V; RTH = 15 × 4.7/19.7 = 3.58 kΩ.

b) IB = (2.39 − 0.7)/(3.58 k + 101 × 0.33 k) = 1.69/36.9 k = 45.7 µA; IC = 4.57 mA; IE = 4.61 mA. VE = 4.61 mA × 330 = 1.52 V; VC = 10 − 4.57 mA × 1.2 k = 4.52 V; VCE = 3.00 V → active.

c) IC(sat) = 10/(1.2 k + 0.33 k) = 6.54 mA, VCE(cutoff) = 10 V.

d) P = VCE × IC = 3.00 × 4.57 mA = 13.7 mW.

e) β = 200: IB = 1.69/(3.58 k + 201 × 0.33 k) = 24.1 µA → IC = 4.82 mA → +5.6%.

f) Want VC = VE + 5 = 6.52 V, so RC = (10 − 6.52)/4.57 mA = 0.76 kΩ → standard 750 Ω.

Solution P5

P7 · Gain in variables (the class assignment) · 5 min

For a CE amplifier with RE bypassed and no load, replace the transistor with the hybrid-pi model (ro ignored) and derive Av = vo/vin in symbols. Explain the sign.

Solution P7

1. The emitter is AC-grounded and rπ sits between the input and ground, so vπ = vin.

2. The current source draws gmvπ from the collector down to ground. That current must come from ground up through RC (VCC is AC ground), so the collector sits below ground: vo = −gmvπRC.

3. Av = vo/vin = −gmRC. With ro: −gm(RC∥ro). Since gm = β/rπ, this is also −βRC/rπ.

The minus sign means the output is inverted (180° out of phase): more base voltage → more collector current → more drop across RC → lower collector voltage.
