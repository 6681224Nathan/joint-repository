# Marketing Strategy Deep-Dive: Social Influence and Segment Dynamics

## 1. Foundations of Social Influence in Marketing

Social influence represents the mechanism by which individual thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are structured by social roles, status, and group expectations. For the marketing strategist, these concepts are the primary drivers of consumer choice and brand signaling.

- **Social Role & Status:** A person’s position within a group is defined by their **role** (expected behaviors/activities) and **status** (relative position within the hierarchy) [2.2Personal Influence_2022.pdf].
- **The Multi-Role Consumer:** Individuals possess **multiple roles** simultaneously—such as being a Brand Manager at work, a Wife and Mother at home, and an avid Fan at a sporting event—each requiring different consumption patterns [2.2Personal Influence_2022.pdf].
- **Status Communication:** Consumers utilize products as semiotic tools to communicate their actual or desired status; for instance, a Vice President occupies a higher status than a Sales Manager and will select products that reflect this relative position [2.2Personal Influence_2022.pdf].
- **Group Norms:** Every social group establishes **norms** (appropriate values and behaviors). Members are expected to comply with these to maintain group membership [2.2Personal Influence_2022.pdf].

## 2. Group Dynamics and Consumer Compliance

Group influence dictates purchasing decisions through a blend of internal psychological triggers and external product-related variables. Compliance is often a result of the pressure to align with established group norms.

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Internal Psychological Drivers [SOURCE_IMAGE_2]|External Product Factors [SOURCE_IMAGE_3]|
|**Bandwagon Effect:** Adopting a style or behavior simply because others have done so, regardless of personal belief.|**Conspicuousness:** The visibility and public nature of a product’s use.|
|**Group Think:** The internal striving for consensus within a specific group setting.|**Complexity:** The technical knowledge required to evaluate or operate the product.|
|**Fear of Exclusion:** The inherent need to be included and avoid being the "odd one out."|**Level of Interest/Involvement:** High cost, value, or personal risk associated with the purchase.|
|**FOMO (Fear Of Missing Out):** Anxiety stemming from a perceived missed opportunity for experiences others are having.|**Prior Information:** The consumer’s existing level of experience and category knowledge.|
|**Desire to be Right:** The inclination to signal social identity by being on the "winning team."||

### Factors Influencing Susceptibility to Reference Groups

Beyond the product and consumer, the **Reference Group** itself dictates the level of influence based on:

- **Nature of the Group:** Frequency of interaction and type of membership.
- **Group Importance:** The degree of pressure to conform and the individual's attitude toward the group.
- **Group Power:** The credibility, attractiveness, and perceived power of the group [SOURCE_IMAGE_3].

## 3. Reference Group Frameworks

**Reference Groups** serve as the essential frame of reference for individuals during the consumption journey. These are classified by the proximity and nature of their influence:

- **Indirect Reference Groups:** These provide a source of information and inspiration regarding desirable lifestyles, typically featuring **Influencers**, **KOLs**, and **Celebrities**.
- **Comparative Reference Groups:** Consisting of **Friends** and **Colleagues**, these serve as a benchmark for specific brand and lifestyle comparisons.
- **Normative Reference Groups:** Primarily the **Family**, these influence foundational values and norms that affect broad product classes rather than specific brands [SOURCE_IMAGE_6].

Strategic dynamics often revolve around **Aspirational Groups**—which consumers **imitate**, **identify with**, and **buy what they buy** (e.g., Michael Jordan)—and **Dissociative Groups**, which individuals "hate to belong" to and actively avoid. Common dissociative examples include "Old people" and "Chavs" [SOURCE_IMAGE_4].

### Case Study: Brand Protection and Dissociative Risk

On August 15, 2011, **Abercrombie & Fitch** (A&F) took the extraordinary step of offering **Michael Sorrentino** ("The Situation" from _Jersey Shore_) $10,000 _not_ to wear their clothing. A&F stated they were "deeply concerned" that his association with the brand was "not brand appropriate" and could be "misconstrued as an endorsement by him of our clothing or—worse—an endorsement by A&F of his wearing our clothing" [SOURCE_IMAGE_6]. This highlights the necessity of protecting brand equity from negative associations.

## 4. The Influencer Ecosystem and Tiered Strategies

Marketing efforts are segmented into influencer tiers to match specific campaign KPIs and strategic objectives.

1. **TopStar (>1M Followers):** Strategy: **Brand Awareness**. KPIs: Impressions, Reach, and Brand Lift [SOURCE_IMAGE_8].
2. **Macro (100K–1M Followers):** Strategy: **Interest**. KPIs: Engagement and Views [SOURCE_IMAGE_8].
3. **Micro (10K–100K Followers):** Strategy: **Conversion (CV)**. KPIs: Direct Conversion [SOURCE_IMAGE_8].
4. **Nano (1K–10K Followers):** Strategy: **Repeat**. KPIs: Brand preference and repeat behavior [SOURCE_IMAGE_8].

### Virtual vs. Human Influencers

Virtual influencers offer distinct strategic advantages by removing the human element:

- **Complete Image Control:** Brands dictate the personality, look, and values to appeal exactly to the target audience [SOURCE_IMAGE_9].
- **Risk Mitigation:** Minimal controversial risk as every action is brand-controlled.
- **Operational Efficiency:** No need for incentive negotiations or sourcing real-world settings [SOURCE_IMAGE_9].

### Affiliate Marketing: Profit Maximization

**Affiliate Marketing** involves monetizing an online presence by sharing unique links. Unlike **Referral Marketing**, which relies on close relationships, affiliate marketing is purely profit-driven and does not require an existing bond between parties. A prime example is the **Shopee Ambassadors Program**, which leverages a network of over **20,000 influencers** and **3,000 brands** to drive passive income through upsized commissions [SOURCE_IMAGE_11].

## 5. Tribal Marketing and Organic Word-of-Mouth (WOM)

A **Tribe** is a collective of individuals joined by shared interests or behaviors—such as vintage car lovers, "cactus lovers," or "skater boys"—rather than demographics [SOURCE_IMAGE_13].

- **The Tribal Strategy:** Brands must identify relevant tribes and address **unmet needs** by "doing what the tribe cannot." For a **Motorcycle Tribe**, a brand should not merely sponsor a meeting but should find experts to help them ride better or discover interesting new locations [SOURCE_IMAGE_13].
- **Organic Word-of-Mouth:** WOM is personal communication between buyers and their circles. It is considered more **authentic, trustworthy, and organic** than paid influence because it is less dependent on the relationship between the company and the speaker [SOURCE_IMAGE_14].

**Case Study: Skinny 'Friend-vertising'** Recognizing that WOM is 83% more effective than standard advertising, the New Zealand telco Skinny launched a "Friend-vertising" campaign. They utilized 46 actual customers in TVCs and geo-located out-of-home ads. Crucially, they achieved localized trust by reaching various communities in **Hindi, Cantonese, Samoan, and Sign language**, allowing customers to generate their own ads for their social circles [SOURCE_IMAGE_14].

## 6. Family and Household Decision-Making

The family serves as the primary unit for passing on consumption values. Decision-making complexity is governed by the **Family Life Cycle** stages: **Bachelorhood**, **Honeymooners**, **Parenthood**, **Post-Parenthood**, and **Dissolution** [SOURCE_IMAGE_29].

### Decision-Making Models by Product Type [SOURCE_IMAGE_15]

- **Husband-Dominant:** Hardware and electronics.
- **Wife-Dominant:** Children’s clothing and groceries.
- **Autonomic (Equal):** Men’s clothing, toys, and games.
- **Syncratic (Jointly):** Vacations, furniture, and cars.

**Socio-economic Status (SES)** also plays a role: the spouse contributing greater resources typically holds more influence, while **middle-class families** are more likely to make joint decisions than upper or lower-class households [SOURCE_IMAGE_15].

## 7. Segment-Specific Strategies: Kids, Teens, and Seniors

### Marketing to Kids

Kids are "influencers of today and consumers of tomorrow."

- **Consumer Profile:** Attracted to fun, animals, characters, and user-friendly designs (e.g., big-handle toothbrushes).
- **Buyer Profile (Parents):** Prioritize safety and products that reduce their "burden."
- **Regulatory Factor:** Governments strictly mandate that adult products must not be targeted at children [SOURCE_IMAGE_29].

### Marketing to Teens (10–17 years)

- **Aspirational Psychology:** Teens "aspire up," imitating those a step or two ahead. Marketers should use older teens in media to drive desire for that lifestyle.
- **Content Strategy:** Due to short attention spans, content must be short, attractive, and constantly updated with new material.
- **Segment Shifts:** Traditional boundaries are fluid; for example, the teenage rock song **"Bad Boy"** unexpectedly became a viral hit among kindergarteners, amassing over 43 million views [SOURCE_IMAGE_32].

### Marketing to the Elderly (65+)

Seniors value independence and family. A high-insight example of product adaptation is marketing **Gerber baby food** to seniors with dentures [SOURCE_IMAGE_33].

- **Retail Needs:** Wheelchair accessibility, rest spots, and menus with nutritional info.
- **Growth Categories (Buy More):** Trips, home renovation, functional drinks, soup, milk, moisturizer, pet food, and rice [SOURCE_IMAGE_33].
- **Declining Categories (Buy Less):** Fashion, electronics, home appliances, instant noodles, MSG, and creamer [SOURCE_IMAGE_33].

## 8. Application of the 4Ps and Distribution in Specific Contexts

Successful marketing strategy synthesizes the 4Ps to accommodate the social and demographic dynamics of the target segment.

- **Price:**
    - **Family Price:** Specific price structures for multi-member households [SOURCE_IMAGE_15].
    - **Premium for Family:** Seniors are cautious spenders for themselves but are willing to pay a "family premium" for children or grandchildren [SOURCE_IMAGE_33].
- **Place (Distribution):**
    - **Digital-Only Channels:** Apps like **TMRW by UOB** target the digital generation with mobile-only solutions and gamification (the "City of TMRW" saving game) [SOURCE_IMAGE_33].
    - **Senior-Friendly Retail:** Physical locations must prioritize accessibility, rest spots, and delivery services [SOURCE_IMAGE_33].
    - **Family Malls:** Large-scale formats designed to serve grandparents, men, kids, pets, and drivers simultaneously [SOURCE_IMAGE_15].
- **Product:** Packaging must adapt to segments, such as single-serving sizes and clear labels for seniors or "Family Size/Packages" for households [SOURCE_IMAGE_15, SOURCE_IMAGE_33].
- **Promotion:** Utilizing "Friend-vertising" and organic WOM to bypass the ad-blocking behaviors prevalent in younger, tech-savvy segments [SOURCE_IMAGE_14].

# Full Course Summary: Strategic Marketing and Information Systems

### 1. Foundations of Marketing Information

In the modern competitive landscape, the ability to translate raw observation into strategic execution is the primary differentiator between market leaders and also-rans. As Hal Varian, Chief Economist at Google, famously noted: _"The ability to take data—to be able to understand it, to process it, to extract value from it, to visualize it, to communicate it—is going to be a hugely important skill in the next decades."_

- **Core Terminology**
    - **Data:** Raw, unprocessed facts that lack inherent meaning on their own. (e.g., A retail scanner records: "1 bag of chips, 12:00 PM, Sunday").
    - **Information:** Data that has been processed, aggregated, and organized into a human-friendly format to allow for interpretation. (e.g., A dashboard showing that every Sunday at noon in the fall, sales of snacks and soda spike).
    - **Insight:** A deep "truth" derived from analyzing information to understand the underlying _why_ of consumer behavior. (e.g., Realizing Sunday peaks occur because customers are rushing to prepare for NFL game kickoffs and prioritize speed and convenience).
- **The Data-to-Action Pipeline** The progression from raw research to business results follows a structured, causal flow: **Research** \rightarrow **Establishing Facts** \rightarrow **Gaining Understanding** \rightarrow **Reaching Insights** \rightarrow **Choosing Actions**
- _Contextual Example:_ A retailer uses **Research** (A/B testing) to **Establish Facts** (identifying that certain stores with front-door "Game Day Bundles" outperform control stores). This leads to **Understanding** the causal effect of cross-merchandising, allowing the firm to reach the **Insight** that bundling chips and soda solves the time-crunched shopper’s problem.
- **Strategic Takeaway:** Information is the "oil" of the 21st century, and analytics is the "combustion engine." Information doesn't just describe the market; it predicts the failure of the 4Ps before they are launched.

### 3. Marketing Information Systems (MIS) and Data Sources

Modern firms leverage three distinct streams of data, each feeding into the strategic planning process. Internal database transaction history, for instance, serves as the primary feeder for demographic segmentation and site analysis.

1. **Internal Databases:** Electronic collections of consumer and market information within the firm’s network.
    - _Pros:_ Low cost; immediate accessibility.
    - _Cons:_ Often incomplete; risks an "inside-out" bias by ignoring external competitive shifts.
2. **Marketing Intelligence:** The systematic collection of publicly available data to stay "in touch" with the market (e.g., tracking competitor promotions or salesperson feedback).
3. **Marketing Research:** A targeted, systematic design to solve a specific business problem or identify an opportunity at a fixed point in time.

### 4. Marketing Research Methods and Problem Definition

The most critical—and most often botched—step in research is the definition of the problem. Strategy consultants must distinguish between the "what" (symptoms) and the "why" (root causes).

- **The Iceberg Principle**
    - **Obvious Measurable Symptoms (Above Water):** Loss of sales, low store traffic, decreasing market share, or high customer churn.
    - **Real Business Problems (Below Water):** Low-quality product durability, unethical treatment of customers, an inappropriate delivery system, or a tarnished brand image.
- **Common Research Applications**
    - **Decision Guidance:** Generating product ideas, estimating demand, identifying market segments, and finding optimal price points.
    - **Result Assessment:** Evaluating marketing efforts, measuring satisfaction, and tracking the transition from interest to actual purchase.
- **Strategic Takeaway:** Addressing symptoms while ignoring the sub-surface problem is the fastest way to erode brand equity.

### 5. Modern Research Tools and Digital Insights

The internet acts as a "Second Life" for consumers, providing real-time, large-scale data that bypasses the biases inherent in traditional surveys.

- **The TikTok "Surprise" Methodology** Consultants use TikTok _before_ having a specific hypothesis to avoid the "circular keyword problem"—the trap of only searching for things you already know exist.
    1. **Search the Category:** Search broad terms (e.g., "cafes," "skincare") to see the unfiltered ecosystem.
    2. **Watch for Signals:** Note unusual engagement (low-quality video with high views), repeated topics across creators, or "comment surprises."
    3. **Mine Comments:** Look for gaps: _"I wish there was..."_ or _"Why doesn't anyone make..."_
    4. **The "Behavior Sentence":** Validate findings with the sentence test: _"People are doing X instead of Y."_
- **Google Trends Mastery: A Strategic Guide** Google Trends validates hypotheses by tracking relative search volume:
    - **High score, stable line:** Established category. High barriers to entry; difficult to differentiate.
    - **High score, rising line:** Scaling trend. Moving fast; the window for action is closing.
    - **Suggested in Related Queries, no score:** Emerging trend below the reporting threshold. High early-mover advantage.
    - **Breakout label (+5000%):** Explosive growth. Verify for 4+ weeks to ensure it isn't just a viral "spike and drop."
    - **No data at all:** Too niche or volume too low. **Strategy:** Switch to TikTok and Wisesight as primary tools.
- **Strategic Takeaway:** Search and social data allow us to move from hypothesis validation to genuine discovery.

### 6. STP Strategy and the 4Ps Marketing Mix

Strategy is the application of information to Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning (STP), which dictates the execution of the 4Ps.

- **STP Application**
    - **Starbucks:** Utilizes population density data for the 15-44 age demographic to determine site viability. Internal transaction history informs which segments are most profitable per location.
    - **Nike:** The "Nike Mind" campaign leverages neuro-data to target segments prioritizing mental readiness over physical metrics.
- **The 4Ps in Practice**
    - **Product:** The Electrolux case (early 1970s) serves as a warning. Positioned as a manufacturer of **inexpensive** vacuums, their UK slogan "Nothing Sucks Like an Electrolux" was brought to America without linguistic research, resulting in a derogatory brand association.
    - **Price & Promotion:** Starbucks integrates weather data to drive "incremental sales." By promoting hot drinks during cold snaps via email, they achieve relevance and high open rates without costly financial incentives.
    - **Distribution:** Big Data and the Internet of Things (IoT) determine physical store locations by analyzing real-time traffic and demographic flow.
- **Strategic Takeaway:** Precision marketing is the antidote to the "one-size-fits-all" failure.

### 7. Consumer Behavior Analysis

Modern analytics allows firms to look "under the hood" of the customer experience to find deep truths.

- **Behavioral Data Tracking (Netflix Case Study)** Netflix analyzes more than just what you watch; they analyze how you watch. Beyond device and location, they track:
    - Browsing/scrolling patterns and pauses.
    - Moments of rewinding or skipping credits.
    - **Data within movies:** Netflix takes screenshots to analyze scenery characteristics, volume levels, and scenery to learn exactly what aesthetic users prefer.
- **Deep Consumer Insight** Insight is a fresh understanding of experiences or beliefs.
    - _Example:_ Research into "Wet Shaving" vs. Electric Shaving revealed that wet shaving is perceived as more masculine because it provides a sense of "complete control and focus."
- **The Modern Customer Journey** The funnel is no longer linear; it is an ecosystem of touchpoints:
    1. **Awareness:** Social/Search ads, loyalty programs.
    2. **Findability:** SEO, PPC, "Near me" queries.
    3. **Reputation:** Reviews and word-of-mouth.
    4. **Conversion:** The purchase moment (App/In-store).
    5. **Advocacy:** Reviews and social posts that fuel the next user's awareness.
- **Strategic Takeaway:** Advocacy isn't the end of the funnel; it's the engine that fuels the next consumer's awareness.

### Distribution, advantages and disadvantages
- direct
	- Pros
	- High quality contact
	- control marketing mi (price promotion, service, quality)
	- Cons
	- ==Smaller coverage==, take longer time to penetrate
	- has fixed cost
	- limited solution
- Indirect channel
	- Pros
	- Large coverage
	- quick penetration time
	- Utilize channel's expertise and resources and knowledge
	- cons
	- High cost, smaller margin
	- ==hard to control ==channel
	- ==reliant on channels==
### When to use what distribution channels?
- direct
	- ==Identifiable customer==
	- product requires ==extensive demonstration==
	- wide ==variety of offerings ==for the target market
	- ==Sufficient resources ==available
	- ==Intermediaries not available== or not capable
- Indirect
	- ==Large no. of customers==
	- ==hard tor reach ==customers
	- product serve market in ==different areas==
	- product used ==heavily, frequently== 
	- ==insufficient resources==
	- ==intermediaries ==perform more ==efficiently== and ==cheaply==

### Levels of intermediary
- 1-level
	- Firm -> retail -> consumer
	- ==durables goods, electronics==, insurance
	- Pros : manufacturers cover ==wide area ==while retaining ==control==
- 2-level
	- Firm -> wholesale -> retail -> consumer
		- ==fast moving consumer goods (FMCG)==
		- markets spread in large area
		- works best if ==retailers== are ==independent ==from each other
	- value of individual purchase is small
	- frequency of purchase is high
- 3-level
	- firm -> ==agent -> wholsaler ==-> retailer ->  consumer
		- Sold by large company
		- ==Wholesalers scattered ==across the country
		- FMCG, garment, durable industry

	- The channel provides ==incremental sales== compared to existing sales
